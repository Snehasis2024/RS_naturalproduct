Natural products: from organism to medicine to machine-learning model

Natural products (NPs) are small molecules made by living organisms, mostly as secondary metabolites. The organism doesn't need them to grow, but they help it defend itself, compete and communicate. This page follows NPs from where they come from and how they are made, through isolation and drug development, to how this repository's model predicts their properties from structure.

Primary vs secondaryPrimary metabolites (sugars, amino acids, lipids) keep cells alive. Secondary metabolites are specialised and differ between species.
Chemically distinctiveCompared with synthetic compounds, NPs are richer in sp³ carbons, stereocentres, oxygen and complex ring systems, and poorer in nitrogen and halogens.
Medically importantA large share of approved small-molecule drugs are natural products, derived from them, or inspired by them (Newman & Cragg, J. Nat. Prod. 2020).

Step 1 · Where they come from

Sources of natural products

Every group of living things produces specialised chemistry. Drug discovery has drawn most heavily on plants and soil microbes, and marine organisms are increasingly important.

🌿
PlantsAlkaloids, terpenoids, flavonoids, phenylpropanoids. Examples: morphine, paclitaxel, artemisinin.
🦠
BacteriaEspecially Streptomyces: polyketides and peptides. Examples: erythromycin, vancomycin, doxorubicin.
🍄
Fungiβ-lactams, statins and immunosuppressants. Examples: penicillin, lovastatin, cyclosporin.
🌊
Marine organismsSponges, tunicates and cone snails. Examples: trabectedin, ziconotide, cytarabine (inspired by sponge nucleosides).
🐍
AnimalsVenoms and hormones. Example: captopril, which was designed from a peptide in the venom of the pit viper Bothrops jararaca.
🧬
Genomes and metagenomesSilent biosynthetic gene clusters found by genome mining (antiSMASH) and expressed in a host organism.
Step 2 · How organisms make them

Biosynthesis: from photosynthesis to specialised metabolites

All natural products are built from a handful of primary-metabolism building blocks. The pathway a compound comes from largely determines its chemical class. Click a coloured product box to jump to that class.

Shikimate pathway

PEP and erythrose-4-phosphate are converted into the aromatic amino acids phenylalanine, tyrosine and tryptophan. Removing ammonia from Phe with the enzyme PAL gives cinnamic acid, the entry point to phenylpropanoids, coumarins, lignans and (together with malonyl-CoA) flavonoids.

Acetate–malonate pathway

Acetyl-CoA is carboxylated to malonyl-CoA. Repeated Claisen condensations by polyketide synthases (PKS) build fatty acids, aromatic polyketides (tetracyclines, anthraquinones) and macrolides (erythromycin).

Mevalonate and MEP pathways

Both produce the C₅ building blocks IPP and DMAPP. Joining them head to tail gives GPP (C₁₀, monoterpenes), FPP (C₁₅, sesquiterpenes), GGPP (C₂₀, diterpenes) and squalene (C₃₀), which leads to triterpenes and steroids.

Amino-acid-derived pathways

Alkaloids form when amino acids (ornithine, lysine, tyrosine, tryptophan, anthranilate) are decarboxylated and cyclised. Examples: tropane alkaloids from ornithine, isoquinolines from tyrosine, indoles from tryptophan.

Non-ribosomal peptides

NRPS assembly lines connect amino acids, including non-proteinogenic and D-amino acids, without using a ribosome. Examples: penicillin (from the ACV tripeptide), vancomycin, cyclosporin.

Tailoring enzymes

After the scaffold is built, P450 oxidases, methyltransferases, glycosyltransferases and halogenases decorate it. These enzymes create most of the structural diversity within each class.

Step 6 · Computation

Predicting natural-product properties with machine learning

This repository's np_model package turns a structure into numbers and learns how those numbers relate to a property, such as compound class, bioactivity or toxicity. The same pipeline handles classification and regression.

Run it yourself

pip install -r requirements.txt

# train (classification detected automatically)
python -m np_model.train \
  --data data/example_natural_products.csv \
  --target compound_class --split scaffold \
  --out models/np_class.joblib

# predict new molecules
python -m np_model.predict \
  --model models/np_class.joblib \
  --smiles "CC(C)=CCC/C(C)=C/CO"

Why a scaffold split matters

Natural products come in families that share a core, such as the flavone nucleus. A random split puts close relatives in both the training and test sets, which makes the model look better than it is. A Bemis–Murcko scaffold split keeps every molecule with the same core in the same fold, so the test measures how well the model handles new chemotypes.

Demo results (69 compounds, 4 classes): 0.94 accuracy with a random 5-fold split and 0.91 with a scaffold 5-fold split.
